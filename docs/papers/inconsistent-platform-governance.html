Inconsistent platform governance and social contagion of misconduct in digital ecosystemsA complementors perspective

Annabelle Gawer (University of Surrey) and Martín Harracá (UNSAM) · Both authors contributed equally

When sellers see a platform enforce its own rules unreliably, and see cheating competitors go unpunished and prosper, their trust in the platform erodes. Some then treat misconduct as a legitimate defence, and it spreads.

§ 1

Introduction: a paradox of power

Digital platforms hold a structural power advantage over complementors, the firms and individuals who sell, build or work through them. Platforms can change the terms of participation unilaterally, exclude complementors and even compete with them. Complementors appear to have little choice but to comply (Cutolo and Kenney, 2021).

Yet complementors breaking platform rules is widely reported: unsafe, fraudulent and counterfeit products and fake reviews on Amazon, account subletting by drivers on Uber, iFood or Deliveroo, manipulation and bots on social media.

Given the overwhelming asymmetry of power, what explains the extent of complementors’ infringement? Rather than starting from the platform, the study starts from the complementors’ experience.

Research question

How do complementors’ experiences of platform governance impact their compliance behaviours?

Finding 1

A spectrum of conduct

Amazon sellers’ practices range from full compliance to repeated, sometimes flagrant infringement, with identifiable drivers.

§ 2

Literature: two views of platform governance

§2.1 · The “efficiency view”

Governance as alignment

Where it comes from
Strategic management and information systems.
Governance is
“The orchestrated use of mechanisms to encourage consistent behavior” (Staub et al., 2023), aligning autonomous actors with system-level goals.
It works when
Rules and roles are precisely defined and enforced “with a firm hand” (Jacobides et al., 2024).
Platform and complementors
A relatively benign, mostly friction-free relationship: platforms succeed when their complementors succeed.
Misconduct is explained by
“Bad actors” (Evans, 2012), a failure to police the ecosystem, or the prohibitive cost of enforcement.
§2.2 · The “power view”

Governance as a battleground

Where it comes from
Strategy, sociology, political science and law.
Governance is
A reflection of structural power asymmetries; the ecosystem is a “battleground” between platform and members (Hunt et al., 2024).
Complementors face
Vulnerability (Curchod et al., 2020) and platform dependence (Cutolo and Kenney, 2021): unilateral changes in terms, arbitrary exclusion.
Platforms hold
Combined executive, legislative and judicial powers, acting as “private courthouses” (Van Loo, 2021), and generate endogenous failures (Jacobides et al., 2024).
Complementors respond with
Power-balancing and counter-power, such as crowd and hacking (Harracá et al., 2023) or “working around the algorithms” (Curchod et al., 2020).
§2.3 · The gap

Despite their power disadvantage, misconduct among complementors persists. The literature offers several explanations, with limited empirical evidence:

  • Mixed compliance incentives: compliance reduces risk, but infringement can pay when competition among complementors is intense.
  • An attempt to redress the power imbalance, at the risk of severe penalties and even deeper dependence.
  • The platform’s limited ability to govern misconduct, or even a lack of incentives to eradicate it (Berzon et al., 2019).

Research methods

3.1

Setting: Amazon Marketplace, 2012–2024

An inductive, grounded study of how third-party sellers experienced Amazon’s governance of its Marketplace. The period opens in 2012, when Amazon launched Global Selling and overhauled Seller Central, including the forum where sellers deal with Amazon and with each other. Sellers based in China were admitted in 2015; by 2024 they accounted for nearly half of the top sellers on Amazon.com.

§ 4

Findings

4.1

Sellers’ compliance and misconduct: White, Grey and Black Hat

Sellers’ practices range from full compliance to flagrant misconduct. The vocabulary of “hats” is the sellers’ own (summarised in the article’s Table 3).

Efforts through which a seller’s participation becomes fully compliant, including treating competing sellers fairly. Compliance is costly: the Marketplace and each additional Amazon service have their own rules, which change often, so it takes experience, training and resources. Most sellers interviewed said they aspire to it, to compete in good faith and to protect themselves from suspension.

Practice examples
  • Ensuring compliance with Amazon’s rulesCompliance verification in all Amazon-related business processes.
  • Keeping updated on policy and rule changesA job position dedicated to policy updates; official and unofficial forums to learn about infringement risks.
  • 41–76%of sellers concerned that Amazon might suspend their account or listings abruptly or for no apparent reason (4 surveys, 2020–2022)

Subtly bending or evading platform rules to obtain minor advantages, exploiting contradictions, ambiguities or omissions without risking a major offence. Because the rules change frequently, Grey Hat practices change too, and they are discussed in unofficial forums, chats and specialised media.

Practice examples
  • Bending image rulesBanners, texts and callouts on product images.
  • Bending wording rulesChanging the prescribed order of words in titles and descriptions.
  • Special characters and emojisEmojis to attract customers; a slash after the first word for search optimisation.
  • Eliciting reviewsA thank-you card asking for an honest review, automated reminders, or asking customers who left seller feedback for a product review.

Activities explicitly prohibited by Amazon, some of them illegal, to promote one’s products, gain cost advantages or harm competitors. Few interviewees admitted using them; many complained about rivals who did. Newness, smallness, lack of reputation and slowing sales appear related to them, and many informants associated geography, particularly China, with higher levels of misconduct.

Practice examples
  • Review farms, rings and “brushing”Paid favourable reviews, or unfavourable ones on competitors’ products.
  • Feedback in exchange for discountsCoupons for future purchases in return for a positive review.
  • Counterfeits and “zombie” accountsKnock-offs; fake accounts that search, buy or return products to sway the algorithm.
  • Listing hijackingEditing a competitor’s unbranded listing to add offensive language, or posting blatantly fake reviews on it, so that Amazon suspends it.
  • 48–66%concerned about Black Hat tactics (2 surveys, 2020–2021)
  • 12–16%admitted engaging in some Black Hat tactic (same surveys)
4.2

Sellers experience Amazon’s governance as inconsistent

§4.2.1 · Declared practices

What Amazon says it does

  • Supports sellers“More than 24,000 Amazonians focused on supporting and inventing for selling partners.”
  • Creates a trustworthy environmentRules, a Seller Code of Conduct and penalties to keep interactions fair and honest.
  • Prevents fraud and abuse“Over $700 million” and “>10,000 people” in 2020 to protect the store.
§4.2.2 · Undeclared practices

What sellers experience

  • Lack of platform supportAutomated, unhelpful answers; staff without authority; cases lost in a “black hole” between departments.
  • Unclear and ever-changing rulesVague wording, frequent updates, parameters discovered only once they apply.
  • Unreliable enforcementSudden, unexplained suspensions alongside poor policing of genuinely fraudulent sellers.

4.3

Sellers’ trust erosion and legitimation of misconduct

§4.3.1 · Erosion of trust
  • Unprotected from rogue sellersAttacks concentrate on the best-selling listings, and protection from Amazon feels absent.
  • Vulnerable to the platform“They [Amazon] will describe it as a partnership, but it should be described as a master-servant relationship.”
  • An inconsistent enforcerFlagrant infringements seem to go unpunished while minor ones attract sanctions that seem excessive. Some sellers give up on the reporting tool.
  • Beyond sellersOne informant became a source for the Wall Street Journal. Nike ended a pilot with Amazon in 2019; Mercedes-Benz and Birkenstock USA also accused Amazon over counterfeits.
§4.3.2 · Legitimation of misconduct
  • An “open secret”Cheating is visible, discussed at events and in forums, and served by a cottage industry of consultants and tools.
  • Self-interested rationalesAmazon earns fees on every transaction; rogue sellers widen selection and lower prices.
  • Defending themselvesFormerly rule-abiding sellers fight back, for example by reporting competitors’ valid images.
  • The only way to competeBreaking the rules, with “plausible deniability”, comes to look necessary.

Contribution 1 · §5.1

Evidencing and theorising platform governance inconsistency

Platform governance theories implicitly assume that a platform owner acts consistently. The study shows a mismatch between declared and undeclared governance and argues that, given the conflicting demands platforms face, such inconsistency is likely to occur. It adds a further kind to Jacobides et al.’s (2024) endogenous ecosystem failures, alongside functional and distributional ones.

Organisational theory helps explain it: decoupling (Meyer and Rowan, 1977), façades (Nystrom and Starbuck, 1984) and organisational hypocrisy (Brunsson, 2007; Wagner et al., 2020). Gaps between words and actions need not be intentional, and the study, without internal Amazon data, cannot say. The pattern is also consistent with Long and Liu (2023): a platform earning both sales commissions and advertising revenue can have incentives to tolerate review manipulation.

Contribution 2 · §5.2

How complementors experience platform power

Abuse of platform power is usually linked to exploitative rules, such as sudden changes or higher fees. Here it is experienced through inconsistent and unpredictable governance. Sellers cannot tell what to expect, and algorithmic enforcement adds opacity, so governance appears capricious. That heightens their sense of vulnerability and erodes trust.

Rule-breaking reads as “hacking” counter-power (Harracá et al., 2023), a way of “working around the algorithm” (Curchod et al., 2020). In line with rational crime theory (Becker, 1968), newcomers with less reputation at stake were more prone to Black Hat tactics. The paradox: by resorting to misconduct to regain agency, complementors take on new risks of detection and punishment, which entrenches their dependence on the platform.

Contribution 3 · §5.3

Social contagion in platform ecosystems

The findings validate theories of social contagion. In social cognitive theory (Bandura, 1986), people learn by observing others: it is complementors’ observation of misconduct, not just its occurrence, that drives contagion, and how blatant it is matters (Gino et al., 2009). Social identity and social network theories predict conformity with group norms and adoption of what one’s network does. Ambiguous rules make transgression easier to justify (Schweitzer and Hsee, 2002).

The study extends this work by showing why digital platforms are particularly prone to contagion among complementors. Their “rules and roles” (Jacobides et al., 2018) give sellers a shared identity, and sellers are highly connected through the platform itself, forums, groups and a cottage industry of service providers.

5.4

Limitations and avenues for further research

Limitations
  • A single platform with durable market power, identified as a “gatekeeper” or as having “strategic market status” by regulators in Europe and the UK, studied over 2012–2024, a period of dominance and relatively low regulatory oversight.
  • Qualitative, self-reported evidence cannot robustly establish causality, and grievances may have been shared selectively.
  • Without internal Amazon data, the study cannot tell coordination problems from intent as the source of inconsistency.
Questions it opens
  • The more opaque a platform’s governance, the more complementors will distrust it. With few exit options, competition, distrust and contagion may intensify; more options to leave would mitigate contagion. Competition from Temu and Shein offers a test.
  • With multi-homing, is there a misconduct contagion effect across platforms?
  • Do the patterns hold on work-intermediation, social media and innovation platforms?
  • Does regulatory scrutiny, which varies across jurisdictions and over time, reduce inconsistent governance and misconduct? Mixed methods and experiments could test the causal links.
5.5

Policy implications

§5.5.1 · Transparency and predictability
  • Hold platforms accountable for detecting and punishing complementor misconduct, such as fake reviews, unsafe products and listing manipulation, with procedural safeguards like timely notice and reasonable investigation, as in credit card dispute procedures (Van Loo, 2021).
  • Require clear, accessible policies, minimum periods before rules change, advance notice of changes, explained metrics and a public record of policy changes.
  • Create independent institutions to monitor platform–complementor relations and audit whether rules are applied consistently and fairly, including access to support staff who can make decisions.
§5.5.2 · Curbing platform power
  • Establish legal frameworks to curb the abuse of platform power, taking its multiple dimensions into account.
  • Consider how a platform’s three functions might be separated, and how counter-powers might be supported and institutionalised.
  • Empower complementors through collective action, representation in the management of ecosystems and a legal framework of rights and responsibilities.

§ 6

Conclusion

Complementors perceive discrepancies between digital platforms’ “talk” and their “walk”. That erodes their trust in the platform and leads them to normalise and legitimise further misconduct among complementors.

Digital platforms are not only “spaces of transactional efficiency”; they are also “digital battlegrounds” (Hunt et al., 2024). Understanding them means leaving behind naïve views of a “platform revolution” of frictionless efficiency, without demonising platforms as purely self-interested agents destroying the social fabric.
